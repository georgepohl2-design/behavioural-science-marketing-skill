# 1. Reciprocity: give first

**In one line:** When someone does something for us, we feel we owe them. So give first, and make it count.

## Why it works
Every human society runs on "you scratch my back, I'll scratch yours". Being in someone's debt feels uncomfortable, so we look for ways to even it up, often with something bigger than what we got.

This is one of the strongest principles of all, because it doesn't feel like a choice. It feels like an **obligation**. You can hear it in language: in Portuguese, "thank you" is *obrigado* (or *obrigada*), which literally means "obliged". Saying thanks is admitting you owe someone. That's how deep reciprocity runs.

Cialdini says the gift works best when it's:
- **Significant.** It's worth something to them, not to you.
- **Personalised.** It's clearly for *them*, not for everyone.
- **Unexpected.** A surprise feels like a gift. Something they expected feels like a transaction.

## Stories from the books
- **Ethiopia repays a 50-year-old debt.** In 1985, while Ethiopia was suffering famine and war, its Red Cross sent $5,000 to help victims of the Mexico City earthquake. Why? Mexico had stood by Ethiopia when Italy invaded in 1935. Half a century later, the debt still felt real.
- **The can of Coke** (Dennis Regan, 1971). In an experiment, a stranger called Joe bought some people a Coke during a break, then asked them to buy raffle tickets. Those who'd had a Coke bought about twice as many tickets, whether or not they liked Joe. The gift beat the relationship.
- **The airport flower.** Cialdini describes Hare Krishna members handing travellers a "gift" flower, then asking for a donation. People who didn't want the flower still felt they owed something, and gave.

## The evidence
- **Restaurant mints** (Strohmetz, Rind, Fisher & Lynn, 2002). One mint with the bill raised tips by about 3%. Two mints raised them by about 14%. One mint, then the waiter turning back with a second "for you nice people", raised them by about 23%. Same sweets, different feeling. ## Famous examples
- **KLM Surprise (2010).** KLM staff looked up passengers who checked in on social media and handed them small personal gifts at the airport, matched to their trip. Significant, personal, unexpected: all three boxes ticked.
- **HubSpot's free tools.** Website Grader, a free CRM, templates and courses. Millions got real value before HubSpot asked for a penny.
- **Free samples** in supermarkets and shops. The oldest reciprocity trick there is.

## Use it in marketing
- Lead with genuinely useful free stuff: a tool, a calculator, a benchmark report, a template.
- **Ungate the good bits.** A "free" ebook behind an 11-field form is a trade, not a gift.
- Surprise existing customers: an unexpected upgrade, a handwritten note, early access.
- In ads, give value in the ad itself (a tip, a laugh, a useful fact) before the CTA.

## Use it in sales
- Open with an insight about *their* business: a teardown, a benchmark, an audit of their site. Not "just checking in".
- Send something useful after the first call before you send the proposal.
- Make the first concession in a negotiation, and name it, so they feel the pull to return it.

## Watch out
- If the strings show, it's a bribe, not a gift. Don't attach the ask to the gift.
- Generic swag (logo pens) isn't significant or personal.
- Over-giving with no ask trains people to take. Give first, then ask clearly.

## Audit questions
1. What does the audience get *before* they're asked for anything?
2. Is it worth something to *them*, and does it feel personal?
3. Is any of it unexpected?
