# 2. Commitment & consistency: get a small yes

**In one line:** Once we've said or done something, we want to stay consistent with it. A small yes now makes a bigger yes later much easier.

## Why it works
We like to see ourselves, and be seen, as consistent. Once someone takes a small step, they start to see themselves as "the kind of person who does this". The next step then feels like keeping their word, not being sold to.

Commitments stick best when they're:
- **Active.** Written, said out loud, clicked. Not just thought.
- **Public.** Others can see it.
- **Effortful.** It took a little work.
- **Freely chosen.** If people feel forced, it doesn't change how they see themselves.

## Stories from the books
- **The prisoner-of-war camps.** In the Korean War, Chinese captors rarely used brute force on American prisoners. Instead they asked for tiny statements, like "America isn't perfect". Then they had prisoners write them down, read them out, and enter essay contests with small prizes. Each small, written, public step shifted how the men saw themselves, and many ended up cooperating far more than they'd ever have agreed to at the start.
- **The toy-shop trick.** Cialdini explains how toy makers advertise one must-have toy before Christmas, then deliberately undersupply it. Parents who've promised it buy other toys instead. Then, in January, the ads return, and parents buy the original toy too, to keep their word.

## The evidence
- **Foot in the door** (Freedman & Fraser, 1966). Homeowners who first agreed to put up a small "be a safe driver" sign were far more likely to later accept a big, ugly "Drive Carefully" board on their lawn: roughly 76% against 17% of those asked cold. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0023552)
- **Restaurant no-shows.** Cialdini tells the story of a restaurant owner who changed "Please call if you have to change your plans" to "*Will* you please call if you have to change your plans?" and then waited for the caller to say yes. No-shows dropped from about 30% to 10%. [Source](https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/)

## Famous examples
- **Duolingo streaks.** Every day you practise, you're more committed to not breaking the chain.
- **LinkedIn's profile strength bar.** Each small step makes the next one feel natural.
- **Pledges and petitions.** A signature today makes a donation tomorrow more likely.

## Use it in marketing
- Break big asks into small steps: a 2-question quiz before the full form, a free plan before paid.
- Multi-step forms: start with the easy, low-risk question.
- Get people to state their goal ("I want to grow pipeline by…"), then tie your offer to it.
- Onboarding: celebrate the first small win so they see themselves as a user.

## Use it in sales
- Agree the problem in their words early ("So the main issue is X, is that right?"). Refer back to it later.
- Ask for small, specific next steps: a 20-minute call, an intro to the finance lead.
- Send a short recap after every call, and ask them to confirm it. Written beats verbal.
- Mutual action plans: both sides write down who does what by when.

## Watch out
- Trapping people (a tiny "yes" that silently commits them to a subscription) is a dark pattern, and increasingly illegal.
- If the first step is too big, you lose them. Make it genuinely small.
- Commitments made under pressure don't stick.

## Audit questions
1. What's the smallest possible first yes here, and is it easy to give?
2. Does the piece ask people to say or write anything in their own words?
3. Does it remind people of something they've already said or done?
