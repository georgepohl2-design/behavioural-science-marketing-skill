# 3. Social proof: show that people like them already do it

**In one line:** When we're not sure what to do, we copy what others do, especially people like us.

## Why it works
Copying the crowd is a shortcut that's usually right. It's strongest when we're **uncertain** (new category, big decision) and when the others are **similar** to us (same job, same town, same situation).

## Stories from the books
- **Canned laughter.** TV producers keep using recorded laughter even though viewers say they hate it. Why? Experiments show it makes people laugh longer and rate the jokes funnier. We take our cue from the crowd, even a fake one.
- **Salting the tip jar.** Bartenders put a few notes in the jar at the start of a shift, so it looks like tipping (and tipping with notes) is what everyone does.
- **"You in the blue jacket."** The research on bystanders (Latané & Darley) shows that in a crowd, everyone waits for someone else to act. Cialdini's advice if you're ever in trouble: pick one person, point, and give them a job. "You in the blue jacket, call an ambulance." Remove the uncertainty and people help.

## The evidence
- **Hotel towels** (Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius, 2008). The standard "help the environment" card got about 35% of guests to reuse towels. "Most guests reuse their towels" got about 44%. "Most guests *who stayed in this room*" got nearly half. The more similar the crowd, the stronger the pull. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1086/586910)
- **UK tax letters** (Behavioural Insights Team with HMRC, 2011–12). Telling late payers that most people in their area pay on time raised payment rates. A single sentence brought in millions of pounds sooner. [Report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fraud-error-and-debt-behavioural-insights-team-paper)
- **The backfire** (Cialdini, 2003). At Petrified Forest National Park, a sign saying *many* visitors take petrified wood led to more theft than a sign that simply asked people not to. "Everyone's doing it" works for bad behaviour too. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8721.01242)

## Famous examples
- **McDonald's "Billions served"** signs: a running tally of everyone who's already eaten there.
- **Apple's white iPod earbuds**: the product advertised itself on every commuter.
- **Booking.com and Amazon**: review scores and "popular choice" tags right where you decide.

## Use it in marketing
- Use numbers that are big *and* specific: "Join 4,327 finance teams", not "Join thousands".
- Match the proof to the reader: logos and quotes from companies their size, in their sector.
- Put proof next to the moment of doubt: reviews beside the price, a quote beside the form.
- User-generated content beats polished testimonials for trust.

## Use it in sales
- "Teams like yours usually start with…" frames the next step as the normal thing to do.
- Case studies from a lookalike company, not your biggest logo.
- Offer a reference call with a similar customer.
- In proposals, show how many similar companies chose the same option.

## Watch out
- **Never** say "most people don't…" about the behaviour you want. It normalises the wrong thing.
- Fake reviews and invented user counts are now illegal in the UK (DMCC Act 2024) and the US (FTC rule, 2024).
- Logos from huge brands can put off small buyers ("not for us"). Similarity beats size.

## Audit questions
1. Is there proof that other people already do this?
2. Are those people similar to this reader?
3. Is the proof placed where the reader hesitates?
