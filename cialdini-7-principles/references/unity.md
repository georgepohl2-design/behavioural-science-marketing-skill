# 7. Unity: make it "we"

**In one line:** We say yes to people we see as part of *us*: our family, our place, our tribe. Unity isn't "they're like me". It's "they're one of us".

## Why it works
Cialdini added unity as the 7th principle in *Pre-Suasion* (2016), then put it in the 2021 edition of *Influence*. Liking is about similarity. Unity is about **shared identity**. We favour our in-group without thinking.

Unity comes from two places:
- **Being together.** Family, hometown, nationality, faith, the same team, "people who do what we do".
- **Acting together.** Doing things in sync, creating something together, going through something together.

## Stories from the books
- **"Because we are Asian, like you."** During the Second World War, Nazi officials pressed Japan to act against the Jewish refugees under its control. Japanese leaders called in Jewish community leaders and asked why the Nazis hated them so much. One rabbi, Shimon Kalisch, answered: because we are Asian, like you. Cialdini tells how that single line of shared identity helped turn the officials against the Nazi demands.
- **Warren Buffett writes to his "family."** In Berkshire Hathaway's 50th-anniversary letter (2015), Buffett had to reassure investors about the company's future after he and Charlie Munger were gone. Before making his case, he wrote that he'd say what he would tell his own family. Cialdini picks this out as unity at work: advice you'd give family is advice people trust.
- **Munger's thank-you share.** Charlie Munger, Buffett's business partner, loved *Influence* so much that he sent Cialdini a share of Berkshire Hathaway stock as a thank-you. The investors who made billions from human behaviour rated this book.

## The evidence
- **Advice, not opinions** (described in *Pre-Suasion*). People asked for their *advice* on a new restaurant concept were more likely to say they'd eat there than people asked for their *opinion*. Advice puts you on the inside, as a co-creator. Opinion keeps you outside, as a critic.

## Famous examples
- **Sport England, "This Girl Can"** (FCB Inferno, 2015). Real women, sweaty and red-faced, exercising for themselves. It said "women like you are already doing this". Sport England's tracking survey estimated 2.8 million women aged 14–40 became more active because of it, and 1.6 million started or restarted exercise.
- **Harley-Davidson Owners Group.** Owning the bike makes you part of a club, and members wear it on their skin.
- **Lay's "Do Us a Flavour".** Fans invent and vote on new crisp flavours. Co-creation turns customers into insiders.
- **Patagonia, "Don't Buy This Jacket"** (2011). Speaks to people who share its values, not just its customers.

## Use it in marketing
- Use "we" and "us" language that names the shared identity ("for people who build things").
- Build community: groups, events, member-only spaces, user conferences.
- Co-create: let customers vote, name, test or design.
- Celebrate local or category pride: "made in Yorkshire", "by marketers, for marketers".

## Use it in sales
- Ask prospects for their *advice* on your product or roadmap, not their opinion.
- Find the genuine shared "we": same industry, same alma mater, same founder journey.
- Customer advisory boards make the biggest accounts feel like co-owners.
- Invite them into something: a peer roundtable, a beta, a community.

## Watch out
- Forced tribalism ("join the #Fam!") from a brand with no real community feels hollow.
- Us-against-them messages can alienate everyone outside the "us".
- Don't claim an identity you don't share.

## Audit questions
1. Does this make the reader feel like an insider, part of a group?
2. Is the "we" genuine and specific?
3. Is there a way for the audience to take part or co-create?
