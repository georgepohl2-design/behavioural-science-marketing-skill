# 6. Scarcity: show what's rare, and what they'll lose

**In one line:** We want things more when they're rare, running out, or slipping away. And we hate losing something more than we like gaining it.

## Why it works
Rare things are usually valuable, so "running out" signals "worth having". Losing access also threatens our freedom to choose, so we push back by wanting it more. Scarcity links closely to **loss aversion** (Kahneman & Tversky): a loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equal gain feels good.

Scarcity is strongest when something is **newly** scarce, and when others are competing for it.

## Stories from the books
- **New Coke** (1985). Coca-Cola's own blind taste tests found people preferred the new, sweeter formula. Then they took the original away, and the public revolted with protests, hoarding and thousands of angry calls. Within three months "Coca-Cola Classic" was back. People didn't want the old taste more. They wanted what they'd lost.
- **The Romeo and Juliet effect** (Driscoll, Davis & Lipetz, 1972). Couples whose parents interfered more in their relationship reported falling *more* in love. Tell people they can't have something, and they want it more.
- **The cookie jar.** See the evidence below: same cookies, fewer in the jar, more desire.

## The evidence
- **The cookie jar** (Worchel, Lee & Adewole, 1975). People rated the same cookies as more desirable from a jar of 2 than a jar of 10, and even more so when the jar had just gone from 10 to 2. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.32.5.906)
- **Lose beats save** (reported by Cialdini, *Harvard Business Review*, 2001). Homeowners told how much money they'd *lose* through poor insulation were more likely to insulate than those told how much they'd *save*. [Article](https://hbr.org/2001/10/harnessing-the-science-of-persuasion)

## Famous examples
- **McDonald's McRib.** "It's back, for a limited time." Disappearing on purpose keeps people coming back.
- **Supreme drops.** Tiny runs, set release times, queues round the block.
- **The cautionary tale: hotel booking sites.** In 2019 the UK CMA got six big booking sites to sign legally binding promises to stop pressure selling and misleading claims. It had found "only 1 room left" messages where more than 50 rooms were available. Scarcity has to be true. [CMA](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/hotel-booking-sites-to-make-major-changes-after-cma-probe)

## Use it in marketing
- Real deadlines: launch pricing that genuinely ends, early-bird tickets, limited editions.
- Frame the cost of waiting: "Every month without X costs you £Y", not "Save £Y".
- Exclusivity: invite-only betas, waitlists, members-only access.
- Make the scarcity specific: "200 seats", "until Friday 5pm", not "while stocks last".

## Use it in sales
- Tie deadlines to something real: price rises, implementation slots, their own deadline ("to be live before your peak season…").
- Quantify the cost of doing nothing in their numbers.
- Share genuinely exclusive information: early access to research or a roadmap preview.

## Watch out
- Fake countdown timers, made-up stock levels and evergreen "last chance" emails: falsely claiming something is only available for a very limited time is a banned practice under UK and EU consumer law.
- Constant scarcity stops being believed. If everything's urgent, nothing is.
- Scarcity can make people anxious, not keen. Pair it with reassurance (easy returns, cancel anytime).

## Audit questions
1. Is there a real reason to act now, not later?
2. Is the cost of waiting, or what they'll lose, made clear?
3. Is every scarcity claim true and checkable?
