# 4. Authority: show credible expertise before you ask

**In one line:** We follow people who clearly know what they're talking about. So show your expertise before you make your case, and make it believable.

## Why it works
Following experts is a sensible shortcut. Real authority has two parts: **expertise** (they know) and **trustworthiness** (they'll tell me the truth). Most marketing shouts about the first and forgets the second.

The quickest way to look trustworthy is to admit a small weakness before your main strength.

## Stories from the books
- **The nurses and the phone call** (Hofling and colleagues, 1966). A stranger rang hospital wards claiming to be a doctor and told nurses to give a patient twice the maximum dose of an unfamiliar drug. 21 of the 22 nurses went to do it before researchers stopped them. A title on the phone overrode training, rules and the label on the bottle.
- **The man in the suit** (Lefkowitz, Blake & Mouton, 1955). When a man crossed against a red light, about three and a half times more pedestrians followed him if he wore a suit and tie than if he wore work clothes. Symbols of authority work even when they mean nothing.
- **The TV doctor selling coffee.** Cialdini tells how actor Robert Young, famous for playing a doctor on TV, sold Sanka decaf coffee by warning about caffeine. He had no medical training. The *look* of authority was enough.

## The evidence
- **Estate agents** (reported by Influence at Work). A London estate agency got its receptionists to introduce agents with their experience ("Let me put you through to Sandra, who has 15 years selling homes in this area"). Valuation appointments rose by about 20% and signed contracts by about 15%, at no cost. [Source](https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/)
- **Admitting a drawback.** Cialdini's research reviews show that conceding a weakness up front makes the strengths that follow more believable.

## Famous examples
- **Avis, "We're No. 2. We try harder."** (DDB, 1962). Admitting they weren't the biggest made the promise of trying harder believable. A classic of trust-led authority.
- **The cautionary tale: Colgate.** In 2007 the UK ASA banned a Colgate ad claiming "more than 80% of dentists recommend Colgate". The survey had let dentists name several brands. Borrowed authority you can't back up will be found out.
- **"I'm not a doctor, but I play one on TV"** (Vicks, 1980s). Proof that the *look* of authority sells, and why regulators now watch it closely.

## Use it in marketing
- Lead with your most credible, specific proof: years, results, qualifications, awards you actually won.
- Publish real expertise: original research, data reports, how-to content that teaches.
- Use named experts and customers with job titles, not "a leading company".
- Admit one honest limitation: "Not the cheapest. Here's why it's worth it."

## Use it in sales
- Let someone else introduce your credentials (a colleague, the intro email, the agenda) so you don't brag.
- Share a relevant insight early in the call. Expertise shown beats expertise claimed.
- Bring in a specialist for the hard questions.
- Be the one who says "you probably don't need our top tier yet". It builds trust for the next sale.

## Watch out
- Fake badges, bought awards and made-up "as seen in" logos. Easy to check, and a breach of advertising rules.
- Jargon isn't authority. Clear beats clever.
- Experts the audience doesn't know or respect add nothing.

## Audit questions
1. Why should the reader believe *this* brand or person? Is that shown, not just claimed?
2. Is the proof specific (numbers, names, credentials) and verifiable?
3. Is there anything that builds trust, like an honest admission?
