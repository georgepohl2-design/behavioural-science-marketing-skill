# Worked example 1: an online hotel booking page

**Input:** a typical hotel listing on a big booking site, the kind everyone has seen. It has a review score ("8.7 Excellent, 2,341 reviews"), "Only 1 room left at this price!", "In high demand: 23 people are looking right now", a "Genius" members' discount, "Free cancellation" and a Reserve button.

*This is a teardown of a familiar, public page format, used to show the skill's output. It isn't a review of any single company.*

---

## Verdict
A persuasion machine: social proof and scarcity are pushed as hard as they'll go, right at the moment of decision. The risk is that the scarcity is the bit regulators have already gone after. If "1 room left" or "23 looking" isn't literally true, this page is a legal problem, not just a design one.

**The job:** hotel listing page · **For:** a traveller comparing 3–4 hotels · **The ask:** click Reserve

## Scorecard
| Principle | Rating | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Reciprocity | ⚠️ Weak | "Free cancellation" feels like a gift, but it's standard. Nothing unexpected. |
| Commitment | ✅ Strong | "Reserve now, pay later" is a tiny first yes. Saved lists and wishlists build commitment before booking. |
| Social proof | ✅ Strong | 2,341 reviews, a numeric score and category scores ("Cleanliness 9.1"). Specific and close to the button. |
| Authority | ➖ Not needed | The platform's brand does the work. Star ratings act as light authority. |
| Liking | ⚠️ Weak | Functional and cluttered. Nothing warm or human. |
| Scarcity | 🚩 Misused risk | "Only 1 room left at this price" and "23 people looking" stack 3 urgency claims. Fine if true. Illegal if not. |
| Unity | ✅ Strong | "Genius" membership makes you an insider with your own prices. |

## 3 fixes
**1. Make every scarcity claim provably true, and say why** (Scarcity)
Show the real reason a price or room is limited.
> Before: "Only 1 room left at this price!"
> After: "1 Deluxe Double left for 14–16 March on our site. Other room types available."

Specific and checkable beats shouty. It keeps the urgency and loses the legal risk.
📖 **The story:** In the cookie-jar study Cialdini describes, the same cookies were rated more desirable from a jar of 2 than a jar of 10. Real scarcity is powerful enough. You don't need to fake it.

**2. Use "guests like you" social proof** (Social proof)
Filter reviews to the traveller's own trip type by default.
> Before: "8.7 Excellent, 2,341 reviews"
> After: "9.2 from 412 couples on a weekend break, like you"

The more similar the crowd, the stronger the pull.
📖 **The story:** In Cialdini's hotel-towel study, "most guests reuse their towels" beat the standard eco-message. "Most guests *who stayed in this room*" did even better. Hotels are where this principle was proved.

**3. Add one unexpected, personal extra** (Reciprocity)
After booking, surprise them with something they didn't expect.
> After: "You're staying 2 nights. We've asked the hotel for a late checkout, free of charge."

Unexpected and personal: it earns the next booking, not just this one.
📖 **The story:** In the restaurant-mints study, one mint with the bill lifted tips by about 3%. The waiter coming back with a second mint "for you nice people" lifted them by about 23%. Same sweets: it was the surprise and the personal touch that counted.

## Integrity check
🚩 Stacked urgency messages are exactly what the UK CMA went after in 2019. Six big booking sites signed binding commitments to stop pressure selling, after the CMA found "only 1 room left" claims where more than 50 rooms were available. Only show numbers you can prove, scoped to what they actually mean. Check with legal or compliance.
